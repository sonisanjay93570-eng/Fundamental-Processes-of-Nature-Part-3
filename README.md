Abstract

Nature exhibits processes at multiple levels, from elementary particle interactions and atomic structure to chemical reactions, biological organization, and observable activity.

This conceptual research framework explores whether the relationships among physical interactions, structural organization, reactions, and observable changes can be studied within a common process-based perspective.

The framework begins with established scientific knowledge concerning electric charge, atomic structure, electromagnetic interactions, chemical bonding, and biochemical processes. It then introduces a philosophical question: should an actor and its action always be understood as two independent starting points, or can their identification sometimes be understood through the organization, interactions, and observable behavior of a natural system?

The proposed framework considers the following conceptual sequence:

Physical Interactions → Atomic and Molecular Organization → Chemical Reactions → Biological Processes → Observable Activity → Identification of the Actor and Action.

This sequence is intended as a general research framework, not as a claim that every natural process follows one universal linear pathway.

The central hypothesis is that actor, action, reaction, and resulting state may be understood as interconnected aspects of organized natural processes.

This hypothesis requires precise definitions, mathematical formulation, comparison with established theories, and independent experimental investigation.

The framework does not claim to replace established physics, chemistry, or biology.

1. Introduction

Scientific investigations frequently examine natural phenomena at different levels.

Physics investigates matter, energy, fundamental interactions, and the structure of physical systems.

Chemistry examines atoms, molecules, chemical bonds, and transformations between chemical states.

Biology investigates living systems, cellular organization, metabolism, regulation, and interactions with the environment.

Although these disciplines address different levels of organization, physical and chemical processes contribute to the operation of biological systems.

This raises a conceptual question:

Can the relationship between a system and its activity be understood through the processes and interactions that establish, maintain, or transform the system?

The present framework explores this question through the concept of Action-Flow Theory.

The purpose is not to assert that all scientific phenomena have already been explained by one principle. Rather, it is to identify relationships that may be clarified through existing scientific knowledge and future research.

2. Fundamental Particles and Electric Charge

Atomic structure provides an important starting point for examining the relationship between physical interactions and chemical processes.

The three familiar subatomic particles have different properties.

2.1 Protons

Protons carry a positive electric charge.

They are constituents of atomic nuclei, and the number of protons determines the chemical identity of an element.

2.2 Electrons

Electrons carry a negative electric charge.

Their quantum states and interactions with atomic nuclei are central to atomic structure, chemical bonding, and many chemical reactions.

2.3 Neutrons

Neutrons have no net electric charge.

They contribute to nuclear structure and stability. Neutrons are not normally the direct agents responsible for chemical bonding, although nuclear properties can influence an atom's characteristics in specific circumstances.

2.4 Scientific distinction

Protons and neutrons are composite particles containing quarks. The strong interaction plays a central role in binding quarks within these particles.

Electromagnetic interactions play a central role in the behavior of charged particles and in ordinary atomic and molecular structure.

These interactions must not be treated as interchangeable.

Research question: How can the distinct roles of fundamental interactions be represented within a consistent description of the processes that lead to atomic and molecular organization?

3. Electromagnetic Interactions and Chemical Bonding

Chemical bonding arises from quantum-mechanical interactions involving electrons and atomic nuclei.

Different bonding arrangements produce different molecular and material properties.

3.1 Ionic bonding

In ionic compounds, electron transfer and electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions contribute to the formation of an organized structure.

For example, sodium chloride consists of sodium and chloride ions arranged in an ionic crystal lattice.

3.2 Covalent bonding

In covalent bonds, atoms share electron density in ways that lower the energy of the resulting molecular arrangement under appropriate conditions.

Covalent bonding is important in molecules such as water, oxygen, and many biological compounds.

3.3 Other interactions

Metallic bonding, hydrogen bonding, van der Waals interactions, and other forms of intermolecular interaction also contribute to the organization and properties of matter.

These phenomena cannot all be reduced to a single simplified picture of positive and negative particles attracting one another. Quantum mechanics and the relevant physical conditions are necessary for a complete explanation.

Conceptual interpretation

The formation of a chemical bond can be studied as a change from one physical arrangement to another.

The resulting structure has properties that depend on the identities of its constituents, their interactions, and the conditions under which the structure forms.

This provides a scientific starting point for investigating the relationship between physical interactions, structural organization, and observable behavior.

4. Chemical Reactions as Transformations of State

A chemical reaction involves the transformation of reactants into products through the rearrangement of atoms and changes in chemical bonding.

Depending on the reaction, energy may be absorbed or released.

Reaction rates and outcomes can depend on temperature, pressure, concentration, catalysts, solvent conditions, and other factors.

A reaction can therefore be studied through several related quantities:

- Initial chemical composition.
- Physical and chemical conditions.
- Reaction mechanism.
- Energy changes.
- Formation of products.
- Observable changes in the system.

A conceptual representation is:

Initial State → Chemical Interaction → Reaction Pathway → New Chemical State

This expression is a simplified representation, not a universal kinetic law.

Actual reaction pathways must be established for the chemical system under investigation.

Connection to Action-Flow Theory

The framework proposes examining an action as an observable process occurring within a physical or chemical system.

Rather than assuming that every action requires an independently defined actor outside the process, researchers can investigate how the system's structure and interactions contribute to the observed activity.

This does not eliminate the need to identify the actual physical agents, reactants, or mechanisms responsible for a reaction.

5. From Chemical Processes to Biological Organization

Living systems depend on coordinated physical, chemical, and biochemical processes.

Examples include:

- Metabolic reactions.
- Protein synthesis.
- Enzyme-catalyzed transformations.
- Transport of ions across cell membranes.
- Cellular energy conversion.
- Genetic information processing.
- Responses to environmental changes.

These activities depend on organized molecular systems.

For example, proteins perform different functions according to their structures, interactions, and cellular environments.

Enzymes can accelerate particular chemical reactions. Membrane proteins can regulate the movement of substances across cellular membranes. Metabolic networks transform matter and energy in ways that help sustain living systems.

Important distinction

The existence of chemical processes does not, by itself, fully explain the origin of life.

How nonliving chemistry gave rise to the first living systems remains an active scientific research question.

Similarly, describing biochemical activity does not automatically explain every aspect of consciousness, subjective experience, or personal identity.

The proposed framework therefore treats biological organization as an important level of investigation without assuming that all biological and mental phenomena have already been explained.

6. Actor, Action, Reaction, and Identity

The central philosophical question of this research concerns the relationship between an actor and its action.

In ordinary language, an actor is commonly understood as an entity that performs an action.

For example:

- A chemical system undergoes a reaction.
- A cell transports ions across its membrane.
- A living organism responds to a stimulus.
- A mechanical system changes its position under an applied force.

In each case, the system and its activity can be described separately for analytical purposes.

However, the system's observable behavior also depends on its structure, internal state, and interactions with its environment.

This leads to three questions.

1. Must the actor always be treated as a conceptually independent starting point before its action?
2. Can the identification of an actor sometimes depend on its organization, behavior, and interactions?
3. Can the actor and its action be studied as related aspects of a single physical process without claiming that they are identical in every respect?

The third question is the central philosophical proposition of this framework.

Proposed hypothesis

An actor and an action may sometimes be understood as distinguishable but interconnected aspects of an organized natural process.

This is a conceptual hypothesis, not an established universal scientific law.

Different systems may require different descriptions. In some cases, the actor can be clearly identified before a particular action occurs. In other cases, the relevant system boundaries and identity may depend on the scientific question being investigated.

7. Natural Balance and Dynamic Change

The concept of natural balance requires careful definition.

In physics and chemistry, equilibrium can refer to specific conditions in which relevant macroscopic quantities remain constant or opposing processes balance according to a defined model.

Biological systems, however, frequently operate far from thermodynamic equilibrium.

Living organisms maintain organized internal conditions through continuous exchanges of matter and energy with their surroundings.

For example, a cell may maintain ion concentration differences across a membrane while ions continue to move through regulated pathways.

The observed stability is therefore compatible with ongoing activity.

This suggests an important research distinction:

Observable stability does not necessarily mean the absence of change.

Within the proposed framework, natural balance may be investigated through the relationship between ongoing processes, constraints, interactions, and the maintenance or transformation of system states.

The term must not be used to imply that every natural interaction produces perfect balance or that every process has a predetermined purpose.

The following sequence summarizes the conceptual structure of this research:

Fundamental Physical Interactions

↓

Atomic and Molecular Organization

↓

Chemical Bonding and Reactions

↓

Biochemical Networks

↓

Biological Organization and Activity

↓

Interaction with the Environment

↓

Observable Changes and System Identification

This sequence is intended to illustrate relationships across levels of organization.

It is not a claim that all biological systems originate through one simple linear chain, nor does it establish a new fundamental interaction.

The individual links are supported to different degrees by established scientific knowledge. The proposal that they can be integrated into a particular general framework remains open to investigation.

9. Research Questions

The following questions may guide future theoretical and experimental work.

Question 1: Physical interactions

How do electromagnetic interactions and quantum-mechanical effects determine the structures and properties of atoms and molecules?

Question 2: Chemical transformation

Under which conditions can a chemical system move from one stable or metastable state to another?

Question 3: Biological organization

How do interacting biochemical networks contribute to the emergence and maintenance of organized cellular activity?

Question 4: System identity

Which measurable properties allow researchers to identify a physical, chemical, or biological system across changes in its state?

Question 5: Actor and action

Can a formal model distinguish the properties of a system from its actions while also representing the causal relationships between them?

Question 6: Natural balance

Under what conditions does a system maintain a stable state, reach equilibrium, or undergo continuing change?

Question 7: Scientific usefulness

Does a process-based description provide predictive or explanatory benefits beyond the established models already used in physics, chemistry, and biology?

These questions should be addressed individually before any broader claim is made.

10. Proposed Method of Investigation

The framework can be developed through the following stages.

Stage 1: Define the terms

Provide operational definitions of:

- System.
- Actor.
- Action.
- Reaction.
- State.
- Interaction.
- Natural balance.
- Identity.

The definitions must be sufficiently precise to distinguish physical quantities from philosophical interpretations.

Stage 2: Select a specific system

Begin with a well-characterized chemical or biological process rather than attempting to explain all of nature at once.

For example, an experimentally documented chemical reaction could provide a suitable starting point.

Stage 3: Establish the accepted scientific model

Identify the established reaction mechanism, relevant physical laws, known variables, and existing experimental measurements.

Stage 4: Formulate a mathematical representation

Define measurable variables and relationships.

For a general system, a state can be represented by a vector:

x(t)

Its evolution may be described by a model of the form:

dx/dt = F(x, u, p)

where:

- x represents the system state.
- t represents time.
- u represents external inputs or environmental conditions.
- p represents relevant parameters.
- F represents the specified dynamical relationships.

This is a general mathematical form, not a newly established equation of nature. The function F must be defined for the selected system using an appropriate physical or chemical model.

Stage 5: Derive a testable prediction

The framework must generate a prediction that can be compared with experimental observations or with predictions from existing models.

Stage 6: Compare results

Evaluate the proposed model against measurements, uncertainty estimates, and established scientific explanations.

Stage 7: Independent verification

Make the methods, assumptions, calculations, and relevant data available for independent examination where practical.

A model should be revised or rejected if its predictions conflict with reliable evidence and cannot be corrected through justified changes.

11. Evidence, Limitations, and Scientific Status

This work combines established scientific background with a proposed philosophical interpretation.

The following distinctions are essential.

Established scientific background includes:

- The electric charges of protons and electrons.
- The electrically neutral net charge of neutrons.
- The role of electromagnetic interactions in atomic and molecular structure.
- Quantum-mechanical explanations of chemical bonding.
- The importance of biochemical processes in living systems.

The proposed conceptual interpretation is:

The identification of an actor and its actions may, in some cases, be examined through the organization, interactions, and observable behavior of the relevant system.

Not established by this work:

- That all natural phenomena have one experimentally demonstrated origin.
- That a new fundamental physical force has been discovered.
- That the framework replaces quantum mechanics, chemistry, or biology.
- That the origin of life or consciousness has been fully explained.
- That a universal mathematical law connecting all these levels has been demonstrated.

No new experimental results are presented in this document.

The framework should therefore be treated as a conceptual research proposal that requires further development.

12. Potential Scientific Value

The potential value of the framework depends on whether it can clarify a specific question or produce a useful model.

Possible areas of investigation include:

- Relationships between physical interactions and chemical transformations.
- Connections between molecular organization and biological function.
- Definitions of system identity during physical or chemical change.
- The distinction between a system, its state, and its observable activity.
- Mathematical descriptions of stability, reaction, and transformation.
- Comparisons between process-based philosophical interpretations and established scientific models.

These are research possibilities rather than demonstrated outcomes.

Any claim of new explanatory or predictive value must be supported by a comparison with existing approaches.

13. Conclusion

Nature can be studied at multiple connected levels, from fundamental physical interactions to atomic structure, chemical reactions, and biological activity.

Established science provides detailed explanations for many of these processes, although important questions remain unresolved.

This work proposes examining the relationship between an actor and its action through the structure, interactions, state, and observable behavior of the relevant system.

Within Action-Flow Theory, the actor and action are considered potentially interconnected aspects of an organized process rather than necessarily independent conceptual starting points.

The central proposition is:

Physical interactions contribute to organization; organized systems undergo processes; processes produce observable changes; and those changes help us investigate and identify the systems involved.

This proposition is a starting point for further conceptual, mathematical, and experimental work. It is not presented as a completed scientific theory or a substitute for established physical laws.

The ultimate assessment must depend on clear definitions, evidence, predictive capability, reproducibility, and independent scientific review.
